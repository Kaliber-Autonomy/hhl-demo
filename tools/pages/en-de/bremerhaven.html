Bremerhaven, nine days out

Our German branch works the first European call on the crossing. Cargo for the German market comes off there rather than riding two more days to Rotterdam and coming back by road.

Why we open here

The ship passes Bremerhaven before it reaches Rotterdam

On the transatlantic services that run this corridor, Bremerhaven is roughly nine days out of Halifax and Rotterdam is about eleven. For a buyer in Bremen, Hamburg or the Ruhr, taking the box off at the German call saves two days at sea and a road leg back across the border.

That is the whole argument for the branch. It is not a bigger port than Rotterdam and we do not pretend otherwise, but for German-bound cargo it is the earlier one, and the customs entry happens in the country where the goods are actually going.

Bremerhaven handled 5.21 million TEU in 2025, which makes it the fourth largest container port in Europe. Container volumes through the Bremen ports rose that year while Hamburg's fell.

German customs

Clearing into Germany is a different job from clearing into the Netherlands

Both are the European Union, so the tariff is the same. What differs is the cash, and it is worth understanding before you pick an entry port.

German import VAT, the Einfuhrumsatzsteuer, is collected at the border by Zoll along with any duty. It runs at 19 per cent, or 7 per cent on reduced-rate goods such as food and books. You get it back: it is deductible as input tax on the VAT return for the period in which you paid it. But you have paid it first, and on a container of high-value goods that is real money sitting with the tax office for a few weeks.

The Netherlands works differently. An Article 23 licence lets an importer account for the import VAT on the periodic return instead of paying it at the frontier, which nets to no cash outlay at all. A company without a Dutch establishment needs a fiscal representative to use it.

So the honest advice is that it depends on your goods and your balance sheet. High-value cargo going to a German buyer who is comfortable financing the VAT for a few weeks should come off at Bremerhaven and save two days. Cargo where the cash matters more than the transit may be better entering through Rotterdam. We will tell you which way round it falls for your shipment, and we are not paid differently either way.

Cargo on this leg

What moves through the German call

Eastbound, out of Canada: seafood for German and Austrian buyers, lumber and pulp, and increasingly aluminium. Canadian aluminium exports rose sharply in a single month this year, led by shipments to the Netherlands, Italy and Greece, and German industrial buyers are in the same market.

Westbound, out of Germany: machinery and machine tools, automotive parts, chemicals, and vehicles. Bremerhaven already handles cars at scale, so a roll-on unit is not an unusual request here.

The German branch

Local people, in the local time zone

The branch exists so that a German importer deals with someone who files into ATLAS every day and answers the phone in German during German business hours.

Halifax runs the Canadian side and the ocean booking. Bremerhaven runs the German customs entry and the inland leg. Rotterdam stays in the picture for cargo that is better entering the Netherlands. It is one file and one invoice whichever way your box goes.
